**COMPANY & BUSINESS BACKGROUND**

> We run a real-world team game played in the city. Players join a team, with friends or with strangers, and meet up for a 2–4 hour gathering in one walkable neighborhood. Each gathering is a set of missions: a hands-on challenge with a local maker, a tasting at a hidden restaurant, a puzzle at a historic spot, a twist nobody saw coming. Teams earn points, levels and badges on a personal web pass, and that record follows them to every future gathering and every city we open. Over a season, the best and most active players earn a spot at an annual weekend flagship retreat.
>
> In simple terms: we turn a city into a game board and a night out into an ongoing adventure. We sell a service (an experience), paid for with tickets, season passes and memberships, plus private runs for companies and sponsorships from districts and tourism groups. We own no venue; local businesses host the missions and are paid to do it.
>
> **Two ways to play: casual by default, tournament by choice**
>
> Every player plays casually unless their team chooses to compete. Nobody is entered into a competition by default, and nobody is ranked unless they opt in. Both kinds of teams play the same gathering on the same night, with the same quests and the same ticket.

- **Casual play (the default):** explore, make things, eat, meet people. Casual players still earn XP, levels and badges on their web pass, and their progress counts toward the flagship through the Explorer path: attending and discovering earns entries into a lottery for flagship spots. Their scores are never shown on a leaderboard.

- **Tournament play (opt-in):** a team enters the season tournament at sign-up or before a season's second session, at no extra cost. Tournament teams are scored on quests completed, speed, accuracy and judged challenges, and appear on the chapter standings. A team can drop back to casual at any time.

> **Tournament structure**

- **1. Regular season:** 4–5 monthly gatherings per chapter; tournament teams build points on the chapter standings.

- **2. Chapter finals:** the top 4 tournament teams play a finals mission at the season's last gathering, in front of everyone at the closing ritual. The winner is crowned Chapter Champion.

- **3. Championship:** Chapter Champions and the highest-scoring runners-up (wildcards) earn spots in the Championship at the annual flagship retreat, which crowns the network champion.

- **4. Explorer weekend:** casual players drawn by lottery attend the same flagship retreat, with their own quests, and never have to compete.

> This follows proven formats: anyone can take part in the CrossFit Open, and the top athletes advance to the CrossFit Games; adult recreational leagues run casual and competitive divisions side by side. Offering both paths grows the audience: competitors bring intensity and stories worth sharing, and casual players make up the larger base that comes for the experience.

**PROBLEM & SOLUTION OVERVIEW**

> **Problem:** Adults in Salt Lake who want to do something new, meet people and see their city have few good options. Markets and festivals are crowded and all alike; escape rooms and scavenger apps are one-off and forgettable; and meeting new people in person is awkward without a shared purpose. Local makers and small businesses, meanwhile, pay for booths or ads to reach customers who browse and leave.
>
> **Solution:** A persistent team game that gives people a reason to show up, a team to belong to, and missions they would never try alone, with progress that makes them come back. Players choose how to play: casually by default, or in an opt-in tournament that runs from monthly gatherings to chapter finals to an annual championship. Local businesses become paid quest hosts and meet engaged customers, and every gathering produces an impact report showing the money spent locally.

> **<u>Key Factors Influencing Success:</u>**

- **A market nobody owns:** our research found no competitor combining in-person teams, local hosts, missions and progress that carries across cities. Timeleft proves the demand for meeting strangers in person (€18M in annual revenue and 150,000 monthly users by April 2026), but it is dinner only; scavenger apps are cheap and one-off.

- **Timing:** Gen Z prefers to spend on experiences over things and is 19% more likely to spend on a day out (GWI), and nearly half play free-to-play games, so game mechanics like levels and badges are familiar.

- **Location:** Salt Lake's walkable neighborhoods (9th & 9th, Central 9th) and dense independent food and maker scene supply the content; Ogden and Provo are a short drive for the next chapters.

- **Low capital:** no venue, no inventory beyond keepsakes; hosts supply the spaces. The pilot needs about \$18,000 in total capital.

- **The owner's background:** food, retail, customer service and finance experience, plus a research-first approach: the pilot is designed to prove one thing (do players come back?) before any money goes into expansion.

- **A proven two-track format:** optional competition on top of a casual base works in fitness (CrossFit Open to CrossFit Games) and adult rec leagues. It lets the game serve both players who want to win and players who just want a great night.

> **<u>Financial Trends:</u>**
>
> Spending is shifting from things to experiences, and prices for comparable experiences support a \$75 ticket. Nationally, guided food walking tours run \$45–\$150 per person (most good ones \$65–\$89), escape rooms average about \$36 per person for one hour, and corporate team-building scavenger hunts run \$50–\$150 per person. Adult summer-camp weekends sell for \$525–\$700, which supports the \$725 flagship price.
>
> Ticketing costs are predictable: Eventbrite charges 3.7% plus \$1.79 per ticket plus 2.9% processing, and by default the buyer pays those fees at checkout, so they do not reduce our margin.

<u>Industry trends:</u>

> The experience economy is growing, and social, in-person formats are among the fastest-growing parts of it (Timeleft reached €18M in annual revenue within 20 months). The gap is between one-off experiences (tours, escape rooms, apps) and ongoing communities (clubs, leagues). Nobody offers an ongoing game played through real places, where your team and record persist. That is the gap this business fills.

<u>Opportunities:</u>

> The business grows in three directions without owning buildings: more chapters (Ogden, then Provo, then Mountain West and national young-professional cities such as Denver, Boise, Austin and Nashville), more buyers per chapter (corporate runs, district and tourism sponsors), and the annual flagship. Growth is gated: a new city opens only after the previous step is proven, and chapters can later be licensed to trained partners using the playbook. The tournament adds growth of its own: chapter finals and the championship are natural sponsor properties (title sponsors, a championship trophy, prize partners) and produce stories that market the next season.

<u>Company and Industry Risks:</u>

- **Return rate:** the model depends on players coming back. The pilot tests this directly, with a target of more than 50% returning, before expansion spending.

- **Host reliability:** a quest host who cancels breaks a session. Mitigation: keep 1–2 backup hosts per neighborhood and pay hosts promptly so they want to return.

- **Food and venue costs:** the finale meal (\$12 per player) is the cost most exposed to price increases; menus are set per session, and the ticket price leaves room.

- **Safety and liability:** players move through public streets and businesses. Mitigation: general liability and event insurance, a code of play, staggered starts and team caps per stop.

- **Copycats:** the concept is easy to describe. The defense is the persistent player record, the host network, and a trademarked brand.

- **Weather and seasonality:** December and June are planned off-months; quests can move indoors.

- **Competition putting off casual players:** casual is the default, casual scores are never public, and both kinds of teams play the same night, so no one feels pushed into competing.

- **Fair play in the tournament:** hosts show a quest's log code only after the quest is done, the game master verifies judged challenges, and teams cannot join the tournament after a season's second session.

> **<u>Covid-19 considerations:</u>**

**OUR BUSINESS MODEL**

> **<u>Value Proposition:</u>**
>
> “We help adults who want more from a night out than dinner and drinks explore their city as a team, through missions, hidden places and local makers, with a player record that carries forward to every gathering and every city, so a night out becomes an adventure they keep coming back to.”

- **Target market:** adults 22–45 in the Salt Lake area (friend groups, newcomers and solo players, gamers and escape-room fans, local-culture seekers), plus companies, business districts and tourism boards.

- **What they buy:** a hosted 2–4 hour team game with 3–4 quests, a shared finale meal, a numbered keepsake badge and a personal web pass.

- **Why they care:** something genuinely new to do, a team to belong to, stories to tell, and status that builds over time.

- **How we differ:** every other option is one-off. Here, teams reunite, quests are never repeated, progress persists across cities, local hosts are paid partners, and players choose their level of competition: casual by default, or an opt-in tournament that leads to an annual championship.

> **<u>Target Market/Demographics:</u>**
>
> Primary players are 22–45-year-old adults in Salt Lake County with disposable income for experiences (escape rooms, food tours, concerts) and a wish to meet people in person. Two core personas: the Strategist (competitive, gamers and escape-room regulars who want standings and a route to the flagship) and the Pathfinder (explorers, creatives and newcomers who want hidden places, makers and friends). Friend groups of 2–6 register together; solo players are placed on teams. In game terms, the Strategist opts into the tournament, and the Pathfinder plays casually and reaches the flagship through the Explorer path. We expect most teams to play casually and a committed minority to compete; the pilot will measure the split.
>
> Business customers: HR and team leads at Salt Lake companies (private runs for up to 20), business improvement districts and tourism boards that want foot traffic and measurable local spending, and brands that want to sponsor a quest or season.
>
> **<u>Marketing Plan for Customer Acquisition and Retention:</u>**

- **Budget:** about \$9,000 in year 1 (≈\$18 per ticketed player) and \$18,000 in year 2.

- **Social content (\$300–\$500/month paid, plus organic):** Instagram and TikTok short videos of teams mid-mission; every gathering produces player photos. Goal: fill the 40-player pilot sessions.

- **Hosts as marketers (no cost):** each host business promotes the session it hosts to its own followers and customers.

- **Community partners (low cost):** run clubs, social clubs, newcomer groups, Meetup and university alumni groups; a group code gets a guaranteed team together.

- **Referrals:** players who bring a new teammate earn a badge and priority for the flagship.

- **B2B outreach (owner time):** direct outreach to HR teams and district associations, using each impact report as proof.

- **Retention:** the game itself is the retention plan. Teams reunite, the next date is announced at the closing ritual, levels only go up, the \$280 Season Pass locks in four gatherings, and progress counts toward the flagship. Tournament teams return to defend their standing and reach the chapter finals; casual players return for their team, new quests and Explorer entries. Target: more than 50% of players return.

Gatherings take place in partner venues and public spaces in one walkable neighborhood per session (starting with 9th & 9th or Central 9th). Service area: Salt Lake County in year 1, Weber County (Ogden) from month 9, and Utah County (Provo) in year 2.

**<u>Product and/or Service Offerings for Revenue Streams:</u>**

- **Tournament entry:** included with any ticket or pass; no extra fee in the pilot. A team entry fee for the championship run can be tested once the tournament is proven.

- **Annual flagship (year 2):** \$725 per player for a 2-night retreat with lodging and meals. It hosts the Championship for tournament qualifiers and an Explorer weekend for casual players drawn by lottery, in the same place.

> **Seasonality:** gatherings run in seasons of 4–5 monthly sessions per city, each ending in chapter finals, with June and December planned off. Corporate demand rises in fall and at year end; the flagship and championship are held in September.

> **<u>Other Key Activities & Metrics to monitor and measure for Success:</u>**

- **Return rate:** more than 50% of players return for gatherings 2, 3 and 4 (the pilot's main test).

- **Quest completion:** more than 85% of teams finish every main quest.

- **Tournament opt-in rate:** share of teams that choose to compete each season; the first season sets the baseline.

- **Return rate by path:** return rates tracked separately for casual and tournament players, to learn which path brings people back.

- **Net Promoter Score:** above 65, from the survey sent the same night.

- **Host re-booking:** all or nearly all hosts want to host again.

- **Local impact:** purchases at host stops and money paid to hosts, reported per session.

- **Unit economics:** ticket price vs. cost per player; marketing cost per new player (target about \$18).

- **Reviews and referrals:** Google reviews after each session; new players referred by existing players.
